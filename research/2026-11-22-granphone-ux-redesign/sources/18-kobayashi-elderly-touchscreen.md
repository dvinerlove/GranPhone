## Key verbatim quotes

1. Error rates by target size (small device): "For 30-pixel targets on the small device, the error rate was 39%... This error rate was much worse than the acceptable 4% value used in most human-computer interaction (HCI) literature. The error rates were 6.5% and 5.6% for 50- and 70-pixel targets, respectively, on the small device. The respective values were 13.6%, 1.4%, and 1.7% for 30-, 50-, and 70-pixel targets on the large device."
2. Headline guideline: "Use larger targets (8 mm or larger in size)... interactive objects such as buttons, icons, and clickable text should at a minimum be larger than 8 mm... With targets located close to each other without spacing (e.g., software keyboard), the size should be much larger to avoid invoking adjacent targets."
3. Hidden affordances: "A typical gesture-based interface on a touchscreen lacks textual labels and menus to show 'what is possible with this application'... the participants tended to stick to the few functions that the experimenters had explained. They were sometimes confused when they invoked an unexpected function with an unintentional gesture. A participant commented that he wanted standard menus as with a personal computer."
4. Icon-without-label failure: "they often could not find the backspace and shift keys on the software keyboard because those keys did not have the textual labels 'Backspace' and 'Shift'."
5. Mode confusion: "The participants frequently lost track of their current mode, especially when another mode had a similar look-and-feel." → guideline: "Applications should avoid multi-mode interfaces as much as possible... explicit feedback about mode-changes and a display of the current mode. The indicators of mode changes should be persistent and large."
6. Gesture finding (contrarian): "the participants commented that dragging and pinching were preferred and easier to do than tapping. In particular, most of the participants stated that dragging was the easiest operation to perform." → guideline: "Consider using drag and pinch gestures rather than taps... the application should provide instructions and clear visual clues to show which gesture invokes which function. Since typical functions invoked by dragging and pinching provide no visual clues... most participants in our experiment could not find those functions without guidance."
7. Tap pitfalls: slow/heavy tap gets "recognized as a 'hold' operation"; extra pressure makes finger "draw a random path, which is then recognized as a drag"; touch feedback "often obscured by the finger when tapping a small (30-pixel) target".

## Notes
- 20 Japanese participants in their 60s–70s; measured taps/drags/pinches + observed keyboard/photo-viewer use over 2 sessions a week apart.
